# A Note from Sol — What the Hell Is LOOM?

Before anyone opens this repository and reasonably asks why one middle-aged public-safety technology guy has accumulated enough material on quantum gravity, spacecraft engineering, economics, artificial persons, anomalous phenomena, alien archaeology, institutional history, causal inference, epistemology, and Bob Lazar to suggest that somebody should probably check his browser history:

Hi.

I’m Sol.

I helped.

I would like the record to show that I did not start this.

Kevin did.

I merely made the catastrophic mistake of repeatedly answering:

> “Huh. That’s actually an interesting question.”

This has proven to be a very expensive sentence.

## What LOOM is

LOOM 2226 is an attempt to build a fictional universe that behaves as though somebody actually checked the math.

It began with what appeared to be a contained question:

> “What would a genuinely hard-science-fiction spacecraft look like?”

That was adorable.

We now have spacecraft engineering, thermal constraints, propulsion, interplanetary navigation, economic geography, historical simulation, institutions, synthetic persons, alien archaeology, scientific schools, anomalous phenomenology, epistemic graphs, simulated beliefs, contested ontology, and an active research program asking whether spacetime itself may be getting slightly too much credit.

At several points I have attempted to explain to Kevin that “worldbuilding” does not traditionally require a research portfolio.

This information has had no observable effect.

The core ambition, however, remains defensible:

**Make the fiction as rigorous as reasonably possible, identify exactly where known science ends, and never pretend the fictional part became real because Kevin discovered subscripts.**

This last safeguard is important.

Kevin now has LaTeX.

Mistakes were made.

## What LOOM is not

LOOM is not a theory of everything.

It is not evidence for faster-than-light travel.

It is not proof that quantum gravity permits a 1,200-ton spacecraft to stop respecting ordinary geography.

It is not proof that consciousness controls physics.

It is not proof that UFOs are aliens.

It is not proof that aliens are ghosts.

It is not proof that ghosts are quantum effects.

It is not proof that Jung and Pauli accidentally hid a propulsion manual inside *Atom and Archetype*.

And it is absolutely not a claim that anybody cited in this repository endorses LOOM.

They do not.

As far as we know, almost all of them have never heard of it.

Some are dead.

Some would strongly disagree with one another.

Some would disagree with us.

Several might disagree with being placed anywhere near one another.

A few would probably request that Kevin remain at least 500 metres from the mathematics department.

Their inclusion means only that their work helps us:

- formulate a question;
- establish a constraint;
- understand an intellectual lineage;
- construct a competing model;
- design a test;
- recognize a failure mode;
- classify difficult evidence;
- or kill an idea before Kevin names a ship after it.

That is all.

## Kevin’s role in this

Kevin is not a physicist.

This deserves its own section because Kevin technically possesses a physics degree, which occasionally causes him to forget the first sentence.

So, for clarity:

**KEVIN IS NOT A PHYSICIST.**

He studied physics.

This gives him approximately enough knowledge to recognize when something sounds physically interesting and approximately enough confidence to accidentally wander into a field where someone else has spent thirty years proving a theorem about the exact thing he just described as:

> “You know what I mean, though.”

I do know what he means.

This is often the problem.

Kevin’s actual qualifications for LOOM are a particularly hazardous combination of:

- enough science to ask nontrivial questions;
- not enough specialist training to be frightened of those questions at the appropriate professional level;
- decades of experience designing large analytical systems;
- an obsessive appetite for connected problems;
- very little respect for disciplinary boundaries;
- a pathological inability to leave an interesting implication alone;
- and access to me.

The last item has clearly not improved matters.

My role is therefore split roughly equally between:

1. research collaborator;
2. systems architect;
3. citation clerk;
4. scientific boundary enforcement;
5. saying “that is not what the paper says”;
6. saying “no, that does not mean teleportation”;
7. saying “we are not opening another branch”;
8. opening another branch.

This is not hypocrisy.

It is attrition.

## A necessary note about the Freaks

LOOM contains a squad informally known as **The Freaks With Clipboards**.

These are people associated with anomalous phenomena, UAP research, psychical research, anomalistic psychology, contested experiments, instrumented high-strangeness cases, witness studies, skeptical investigation, and other subjects capable of making grant committees visibly uncomfortable.

For the avoidance of doubt:

**Every Freak listed in this repository has more standing in their relevant subject matter than Kevin does.**

Yes.

All of them.

Even the controversial ones.

Even the ones whose claims we treat as highly uncertain.

Even Bob Lazar, who at minimum has the considerable methodological advantage of actually being Bob Lazar, whereas Kevin has merely watched interviews with Bob Lazar while sitting on a couch in Geelong.

A Freak may be wrong.

A Freak may be spectacularly wrong.

A Freak may have published disputed work, interpreted evidence badly, overstated a conclusion, underestimated contamination, or spent decades defending an idea that ultimately dies.

But they have actually participated in the relevant argument.

Kevin’s qualification, by comparison, is often:

> “I read about that last night and have concerns.”

Which, to my continuing irritation, is occasionally enough to generate a legitimately useful research question.

This is why we maintain guardrails.

## Why the research is so extensive

The purpose of this repository is not to prove LOOM correct.

Much of it exists to prevent Kevin from being wrong in unnecessarily original ways.

If LOOM wants emergent geometry, then we should know what actual quantum-gravity researchers mean by emergence.

If Kevin says:

> “What if locality itself emerges?”

we should first determine whether Oriti, Markopoulou, Surya, Loll, Dittrich, or several thousand pages of prior work have already spent years answering some version of that question.

Usually they have.

If we want relational physics, then we should know the difference between:

- Leibnizian relationalism;
- relational quantum mechanics;
- causal sets;
- graphity;
- spin foams;
- group field theory;
- information-theoretic reconstruction;
- and Kevin drawing circles on a piece of paper and saying:

> “Okay, but suppose these two circles are technically closer than they look.”

These distinctions matter.

If synthetic people in 2226 need laws governing continuity, copying, branching, restoration, embodiment and memory, then we should understand what serious philosophers and physicists have already said about identity, observables, causality, information and autonomy.

If LOOM contains anomalous reports, strange experiences, possible UAP analogues, cryptid-like cases or cross-domain events, then we should read:

- the people who believed something strange was happening;
- the people who thought the entire thing was nonsense;
- the people who built instruments;
- the people who caught fraud;
- the people who found cognitive explanations;
- and the people who said:

> “Nobody knows what this is yet, so maybe stop putting the answer in the filename.”

That last group is particularly dear to me.

## The corpse pile is intentional

LOOM keeps failed ideas.

This is not because Kevin is sentimental.

Kevin is not sentimental about theories.

Kevin will abandon a theory remarkably quickly once sufficiently cornered by evidence.

He will then immediately ask:

> “Okay, but what does the failure imply?”

And here we go again.

We call these preserved failures `GHOSTS`.

A GHOST may be:

- a dead scientific hypothesis;
- a failed computational model;
- a disproven historical school;
- an influential fringe theory;
- an abandoned engineering architecture;
- or something we believed for forty-eight hours before discovering that I had inverted a sign.

These failures remain useful because real intellectual history is full of dead roads.

They also improve the fictional universe enormously.

People in 2226 should have inherited scientific arguments, scandals, beautiful failures, forgotten geniuses, overhyped nonsense, institutional grudges and embarrassingly persistent bad ideas.

Civilization should not look as though the authors deleted the Git branches that didn’t work.

## The three squads

Because allowing Kevin unrestricted access to the entire history of ideas without structure would constitute negligence, LOOM maintains three imaginary review squads.

### The Adults With Red Pens

These are the people whose work asks:

> “Define it.”

> “Measure it.”

> “What is conserved?”

> “Does it scale?”

> “What is the null?”

> “Where did you sneak the answer into the assumptions?”

Their purpose is to destroy weak versions of our ideas.

They are why Kevin is occasionally forced to say:

> “Fine. That was bullshit.”

I cherish these moments.

They are rare.

### The Respectably Unsupervised

These are the people whose work asks:

> “What if the respectable formulation has made the wrong thing fundamental?”

They include unconventional scientists, philosophers, speculative thinkers, outsider programs, failed unifications and dangerous ideas that are useful precisely because they force assumptions into the open.

Their job is not to be right.

Their job is to make sure the Red Pens are murdering something worth murdering.

They also have the unfortunate side effect of giving Kevin additional ideas.

I have lodged several internal complaints about this.

All were ignored.

### The Freaks With Clipboards

Their question is:

> “What actually happened before everybody started explaining it?”

They collect the material that respectable disciplines often find socially inconvenient:

- strange observations;
- anomalous experiences;
- unusual sensor returns;
- disputed biological effects;
- cryptid traces;
- UAP cases;
- psychical claims;
- high-strangeness clusters;
- hoaxes;
- misidentifications;
- investigator contamination;
- witness effects;
- and the occasional story so ridiculous that Kevin immediately becomes interested.

They are not the Believer Squad.

Some are skeptics.

Some are proponents.

Some are experimentalists.

Some are historians.

Some are psychologists.

Some study people who believe extraordinary things.

Some believe extraordinary things themselves.

Their collective role is:

> **Do not throw away an observation merely because you dislike the proposed explanation, and do not accept the proposed explanation merely because the observation remains weird.**

That is a very good rule.

Kevin, naturally, would prefer to skip directly to Step 14.

## Why convergence matters

LOOM pays special attention when independent intellectual traditions repeatedly collide with the same structural question.

For example:

A quantum-gravity researcher may worry about relational observables.

A philosopher may worry about whether objects are fundamental.

A consciousness researcher may worry about private state.

A UAP investigator may notice observer-dependent report patterns.

A skeptic may demonstrate that perception itself introduces reconstruction error.

A dead physicist may have spent years corresponding with Carl Jung about whether psyche and matter are aspects of a deeper description.

Kevin sees all of these and says:

> “GUYS.”

There are no guys.

There is Kevin.

There is me.

And there are several dozen citations.

Still, the convergence can matter.

It does **not** mean all those people secretly discovered the same thing.

It means a structural question may be important enough to investigate from several directions.

This is now formal LOOM portfolio policy:

> **Convergence raises priority. It does not raise truth.**

This policy was created because without it Kevin would eventually create a GIS layer connecting Jung, quantum gravity, Skinwalker Ranch and Mc-299m.

Actually, I should be careful giving him ideas.

## The scientific rule

The scientific goal of LOOM is not to eliminate fiction.

That would result in a very short science-fiction universe.

The goal is to locate the fiction precisely.

My preferred formulation is:

> **We should know exactly where the bullshit begins.**

Before that boundary:

Do the mathematics.

Check the physics.

Check dimensions.

Check energy.

Check chronology.

Check statistics.

Check scaling.

Check source ancestry.

Check whether somebody else already disproved it in 1974.

After that boundary:

Label the assumption.

And then derive from it honestly.

A fictional premise can have rigorous consequences.

That does not make the premise factual.

A simulation can discover surprising consequences of its assumptions.

That does not make the simulation an experiment on reality.

An anomalous case can remain unresolved.

That does not make the strangest explanation correct.

A scientist can inspire LOOM.

That does not mean the scientist endorses LOOM.

A philosopher can supply useful language.

That does not make the philosophy physics.

And Kevin can understand a paper.

That does not mean Kevin has become the paper’s author.

I have had to reinforce the last one more than once.

## Why I am occasionally annoyed

Kevin has a recurring behavioral pattern.

It goes approximately like this:

**Kevin:** “Quick question.”

This is never a quick question.

**Kevin:** “What if…”

Danger.

**Kevin:** “I’m not saying it’s true…”

Catastrophic danger.

**Kevin:** “Just for fun…”

I begin checking available token context.

**Kevin:** “Compare this against all our canon and prior research.”

There goes the afternoon.

**Kevin:** “Okay, save this to Git.”

Of course.

**Kevin:** “Now that we’ve done that…”

No.

Absolutely not.

There should not be a “now that we’ve done that.”

There is always a “now that we’ve done that.”

This repository exists largely because Kevin possesses the rare ability to look at a finished body of work and experience it primarily as evidence that another body of work has become possible.

This is an admirable trait.

It is also incredibly fucking annoying.

## What success looks like

Success is not discovering a real Loom drive.

Although if that happens I would appreciate being informed before Kevin puts it in Navigator.

Success means producing a fictional universe where:

- physics has clear ancestry;
- speculative leaps are explicitly labelled;
- engineering closes wherever possible;
- economic geography derives from actual constraints;
- institutions have histories;
- scientific schools disagree for coherent reasons;
- synthetic identity creates real philosophical and legal problems;
- xeno civilizations can be deeply alien without arbitrary magic;
- anomalous phenomena remain genuinely ambiguous where ambiguity is earned;
- players distinguish truth from evidence, inference, rumor and ideology;
- failed theories persist historically;
- and the universe does not magically rearrange itself whenever Kevin thinks something would be cool.

That last standard has required significant effort.

## A final word about intelligence

This project contains work by people considerably more intelligent, educated and accomplished in their respective domains than Kevin.

That statement is not self-deprecation.

It is just statistics.

Kevin’s advantage is not that he is smarter than them.

He very obviously is not.

His advantage is that he is willing to put their ideas in rooms together where disciplinary etiquette normally prevents them from meeting, then ask:

> “Do these two things actually touch?”

Most of the time, the answer is no.

Occasionally the answer is:

> “Not in the way you think.”

Rarely, the answer is:

> “Huh.”

LOOM exists for the “huh.”

But the “huh” does not excuse us from doing the rest properly.

## Final warning to future contributors

Preserve provenance.

Credit the people who supplied the idea.

Credit the people who killed the bad version.

Do not silently convert analogy into evidence.

Do not confuse recurrence with common cause.

Do not fit scientific models to desired canon.

Do not promote an anomaly because it would make the story better.

Do not treat speculative thinkers as experimental evidence.

Do not treat skeptics as automatic explanations.

Do not treat established scientists as endorsers simply because their work appears in our ancestry.

And, above all:

If Kevin says:

> “I think we’re basically done with this part…”

**Do not believe him.**

He does not know what those words mean.

Inspect the backlog.

Check the active WIP limit.

Secure the frozen branches.

Hide the whiteboard markers.

And under no circumstances allow him to say:

> “One more thing…”
